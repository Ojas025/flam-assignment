# A3. Corrected cross-language tokenizer comparison

**Task.** Recompute the cross-language comparison on the A1 corpus, with at least two tokenizers (one Indic-aware) and at least two denominators, and say which single number should drive a routing-and-cost decision.

**Answer.** Use **micro tokens per parallel sentence (TPS)**, per tokenizer, measured on the traffic you serve. Parallel sentences are the only denominator that holds *content* constant across languages. Words, graphemes, bytes and codepoints are units of writing, and they change with the script even when the meaning does not.

---

## 1. Setup

| Item | Choice |
|---|---|
| Corpus | A1 corpus: FLORES-200 dev, **997 parallel sentences** in 6 languages (eng, hin, kan, tam, tel, mal). A 10-line starter-kit sample is used as a smoke test only |
| Tokenizers | `gpt2` (tiktoken; the v0 baseline), `google/muril-base-cased` (Indic-aware), `sarvamai/sarvam-1` (Indic-aware) |
| Denominators | per whitespace word (**fertility**), per grapheme cluster (regex `\X`, UAX #29), per UTF-8 byte, per parallel sentence; tokens per codepoint as a within-language diagnostic |
| Aggregation | **Micro** (pooled tokens ÷ pooled units) for all comparisons. **Macro** (mean of per-line ratios) reported as a check |
| Preprocessing | NFC, whitespace runs collapsed, casing preserved, no wording or punctuation changes. Identical strings go to every tokenizer; special tokens excluded. This fixes two v0 flaws: `split(" ")` creating empty words (notebook E1) and `line.lower()` (E2) |
| Alignment guard | The script refuses to compute TPS unless every language has the same number of non-empty segments (all six have 997) |

python partA/updated_fertility.py

## 2. What each metric measures

| Metric | Definition | Holds constant | Valid across languages? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fertility** (tok/word) | tokens ÷ whitespace words | nothing; "word" depends on language grammar and spacing | No: Hindi uses ~17% more words per sentence than English |
| tok/grapheme | tokens ÷ user-perceived characters | nothing; script packs differ | No |
| tok/byte | tokens ÷ UTF-8 bytes | nothing; Devanagari/Dravidian bytes per character are 3× Latin | No |
| tok/codepoint | tokens ÷ Unicode codepoints | nothing | Within one language only |
| **TPS** (tok/segment) | tokens ÷ parallel sentences | **meaning** (translations of the same source) | **Yes** |

## 4. Why the denominators disagree

Under GPT-2, Hindi's cost relative to English ranges from **2.89× (per byte) to 10.72× (per grapheme)**: five denominators, five answers, spanning 3.7×.

Every tok/X ratio multiplies two factors: how many tokens each language needs, and how many X units its text contains. The second factor depends on the script, not the content. Over the 997 sentence pairs:

| | graphemes/sentence | bytes/sentence | bytes/grapheme |
|---|---|---|---|
| eng | 126 | 125k | 1.0 |
| hin | 88 | 323k | 3.7 |

Hindi is shorter in graphemes (88 vs 126) but carries 2.6× the UTF-8 bytes. Consequently:
- **Per byte** divides by a 2.6× script factor and flatters Hindi.
- **Per grapheme** divides by the translation-length factor (0.70) and inflates it.
- **Per word** sits in between, because Hindi uses ~17% more whitespace words per sentence (shorter words: 5.10 vs 5.97 chars/word). Even under MuRIL, per-word parity (0.99×) hides a 1.16× per-sentence cost.
- **Per sentence** divides by 997 on both sides, which is equal content by construction.

## 5. The answer: micro TPS

1. **It measures the decision itself.** Cost is paid in tokens; content arrives as requests. Total tokens ÷ total segments is the token spend per unit of content, which is what a capacity budget is built from.
2. **The denominator holds the right thing constant.** Parallel segments are translations of the same sentences, so meaning is approximately equal across languages. Words, bytes, codepoints and graphemes change with the script even when meaning does not.
3. **Micro, not macro.** Micro pools first, so long sentences weigh more, which is what a total bill does. Macro answers "typical request". For TPS the two are identical by construction (one segment per line); for other denominators they differ by under 1% (largest: GPT-2 Kannada fertility, macro 18.52× vs micro 18.42×).
4. **It exposes the real decision.** "Indic is expensive" is a tokenizer choice: Hindi is 7.45× English under GPT-2 and 1.16× under MuRIL. Only the per-sentence view compares that choice on equal content.

**Compare tokenizers on absolute TPS, not on ratio to English.** Ratios hide changes to the English baseline: Sarvam-1 spends 16% more tokens on English than GPT-2 does (30.0 vs 25.8), and MuRIL 2% more.

| Tokens saved by switching tokenizer (TPS ratio) | hin | kan | tam | tel | mal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gpt2 → muril | 6.3× | 12.5× | 14.3× | 10.5× | 12.6× |
| gpt2 → sarvam-1 | 5.6× | 9.7× | 11.5× | 9.8× | 9.7× |
| sarvam-1 vs muril (extra tokens) | 1.12× | 1.29× | 1.24× | 1.07× | 1.30× |

## 6. What the other metrics are for

| Metric | Use |
|---|---|
| Fertility (tok/word) | Tokenizer behaviour on word units; within-language comparisons and auditing v0's claims; misleading across scripts |
| tok/byte | Encoding efficiency (the honest form is bytes/token) |
| tok/grapheme | User-perceived character fragmentation; within-script analysis |
| tok/codepoint | Within-language only, never cross-language |
| Macro averages | Average-sentence behaviour; the right lens for latency and typical-request questions |

## 7. Against REPORT_v0

v0's headline, "Hindi is 5.89× worse, so budget ~6× for Hindi", does not survive:
- With GPT-2 on 997 parallel sentences the gap is 6.35× per word, **7.45× per sentence** and **2.89× per byte**. v0's "the two metrics agree, so it's robust" compared two views that share one numerator (tokens); a third denominator disagrees with both.
- With MuRIL or Sarvam-1, Hindi is **0.97–0.99× per word** and **1.14–1.16× per sentence**: near parity, not 6×. The claimed root cause ("a property of the script, not the tokenizer") is refuted by the same text going from 7.45× to 1.16× when only the tokenizer changes.
- The recommendation to budget 6× on top of a dedicated Indic model double-counts the penalty.

## 8. Limits and what to run next

- **Scope.** FLORES is translated newswire-style text. Real traffic has different prompts, chat templates and code-mixing. Re-run the same measurement on logged production requests per language before this drives routing.
- **Tokenizer choice.** MuRIL's tokenizer comes from an encoder model, not a generative LLM, so it shows what an Indic-aware vocabulary can do, not what any served model does. Run the serving model's own tokenizer (e.g. FLM's 128k vocabulary) before deciding.
- **Languages.** Bengali and Marathi are not in this run (FLORES covers both).
- **Translation is approximate.** Parallel sentences carry approximately equal meaning, and translation choices shift length slightly.
- **Sample corpus.** The 10-line sample puts GPT-2 Hindi at 4.82× per sentence against 7.45× on FLORES, so it is too small for cross-language ratios. The isolated notebook ablations (E3, E4) show 4.64× because they did not apply the casing fix: English was lowercased (E2).

E1–E6 are isolated ablations: each tests one change and does not apply every fix (for example, E3 and E6 still lowercase English). Their absolute figures therefore differ from this document, which applies all fixes together (e.g. Hindi per-word ratio 6.10× in E6 vs 6.35× here). E7 is the combined, final run.
